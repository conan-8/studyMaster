# Archetype: Evaluating Claims

- **Slug:** `evaluating-claims`
- **Taxonomy:** `SAT_MATH:evaluating-claims` (Problem-Solving and Data Analysis)
- **Stimulus:** prose study summary (`passage`, 45–110 words) — who was studied, how chosen,
  whether groups were assigned, sizes, and the drawn conclusion; **no diagram**
- **Answer mode:** which-statement — `mcq` only; choices are conclusions/critiques

## What the skill tests

Matching a study's conclusions to what its design licenses. Two questions govern every
item: **were treatments randomly assigned?** (licenses causal claims) and **who was
randomly sampled?** (licenses generalization to exactly that population). Observational
data support association wording only; a voluntary poll of 100,000 is weaker evidence
than a random sample of 500; size never cures selection bias.

## Statement-distractor pattern (generator-critical)

No numeric distractors here: each wrong choice is a named misconception expressed as a
study-design claim. Copy the key's sentence shape and mutate exactly one element —
swap the verb to causal, widen the population, cite the wrong randomization as
justification, or lean on sample size as proof of validity.

## Worked example (medium)

**Stimulus.** A researcher randomly sampled 300 students at Lincoln High School and
surveyed them about sleep. Students who reported sleeping 8 or more hours per night
had higher grade-point averages than students who reported sleeping less. A local
headline read: "Extra sleep causes better grades at Lincoln High."

**Stem.** Which of the following conclusions is best supported by the study?

- A) Students in the sample who slept 8 or more hours per night tended to have higher
  GPAs than students who slept less.
- B) Sleeping 8 or more hours per night causes higher GPAs at Lincoln High School.
- C) Students who sleep 8 or more hours per night tend to have higher GPAs than other
  students nationwide.
- D) Extra sleep causes higher GPAs at Lincoln High School because the researcher
  randomly sampled the students.

**Key:** A. The study observed an association in a random sample; no treatments were
assigned, and the sample covers only Lincoln High — so an associational claim about the
sampled students is exactly what is licensed.

**Rationale (each distractor names its misconception):**

- **B** — `SAT_MATH:evaluating-claims-causal-claim-from-observational-study`: converts
  the observed association into causation; students chose their own sleep amounts.
- **C** — `SAT_MATH:evaluating-claims-generalizing-beyond-sampled-population`: extends
  the result from one school's random sample to students nationwide.
- **D** — `SAT_MATH:evaluating-claims-random-sampling-vs-random-assignment-swap`:
  reaches the causal claim (as in B) and justifies it by the random **sampling**, which
  licenses generalization — not causation.

## Generation notes

- Build the design before the claim: state selection method, assignment (or its
  absence), and sizes (random samples 50–500; voluntary polls 10,000+).
- The passage must fully determine the answer — both licensing questions answerable
  from the text alone; no outside statistics knowledge.
- Distractors = one mutation each: verb class, population scope, cited justification,
  size-cures-bias. Match sentence length and register across all four choices.
- Hard band: two-dimensional discrimination (right strength/wrong scope AND right
  scope/wrong strength among choices) and matched-cohort studies that merely mimic
  experiments.
