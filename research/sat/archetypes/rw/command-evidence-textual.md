# Command of Evidence: Textual

**Slug:** `command-evidence-textual` · **Domain:** Information and Ideas

## What it tests

Whether the student can judge which *finding or quotation* would most
directly support (or weaken) a claim stated in the passage. The passage sets
up a research context and ends in an explicit hypothesis or claim; the
reasoning work is matching evidence to the claim's exact logical shape —
its variables, direction, population, and conditions.

## Worked shape

One to three sentences of science or social-science context, ending in a
claim (often followed by a blank). The stem is typically *"Which finding, if
true, would most directly support [the] claim?"* The four choices are
plausible findings in the scenario. The key instantiates the claim's logic
exactly; distractors are on-topic but off-mechanism, off-scope, reversed in
direction, or mere restatements of the claim.

## Original example item (medium difficulty)

> Ecologist Renata Okafor hypothesized that the wildflower *Lupinus
> rivularis* attracts more pollinators in partial shade than in full sun
> because shade slows nectar evaporation, keeping flowers rewarding to
> visitors for longer each day.

**Which finding, if true, would most directly support Okafor's hypothesis?**

- A) *Lupinus rivularis* plants grown in greenhouses flower earlier than
  plants grown outdoors.
- B) In a field survey, partially shaded *L. rivularis* flowers retained
  nectar volume longer into the afternoon and received more pollinator
  visits than flowers in full sun. ✔
- C) Several wildflower species unrelated to *L. rivularis* are pollinated
  primarily by beetles rather than by bees.
- D) Nectar composition in *L. rivularis* does not vary between plants in
  sun and plants in shade.

**Why the distractors fail:** A is about flowering time, not the nectar /
pollinator mechanism. C changes the species and the question. D reports a
null result about nectar *composition*, which neither supports nor tests the
claim about nectar *volume* and visits.

## Difficulty commentary

- **Easy:** simple causal claim; one choice obviously tests that exact link;
  distractors irrelevant or reversed.
- **Medium:** the claim carries a comparison or qualifier ("more than",
  "only when") that the evidence must match; distractors are on-mechanism
  but off-scope.
- **Hard:** the claim is conditional or mechanistic, and multiple choices
  point in the "right direction" — only one isolates the claimed variable.
  Weaken variants where the best weakener attacks an assumption are the
  hardest form.

## Generation pitfalls

1. **Vague claims.** If the claim's logical shape (causal? comparative?
   conditional?) is not explicit, "most direct support" becomes a matter of
   taste. Write the claim so its shape can be underlined.
2. **Evidence that merely coheres.** A finding can be consistent with the
   claim yet fail to support it (it fits rival explanations equally well).
   The key must discriminate.
3. **Distractors that need outside knowledge to rule out.** The student
   should reject each distractor by logic against the claim, not by knowing
   the field.
4. **Restatement distractors that are too obvious.** A choice that just
   re-says the claim is fine as a trap only if dressed as a "finding"; make
   it earn its plausibility.
