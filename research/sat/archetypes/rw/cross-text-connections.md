# Cross-Text Connections

**Slug:** `cross-text-connections` · **Domain:** Craft and Structure

## What it tests

Whether the student can hold two short texts' stances in mind at once and
characterize how they relate: how one author would respond to the other's
claim, what the relationship between the texts is, or where the authors
would agree. The discriminator is almost always *stance-tracking precision* —
which author holds which position, with which qualifiers.

## Worked shape

Two labeled texts, each 1–3 sentences (roughly 30–80 words), on a shared
topic. Text 1 carries an explicit, quotable claim. Text 2 relates to it in
exactly one way: challenges it, complicates it, supports it with new
evidence, or explains it differently. Stems include *"How would the author
of Text 2 most likely respond to the claim in Text 1?"* and *"Which choice
best describes the relationship between the texts?"* The correct answer must
be verified against *both* texts independently.

## Original example item (medium difficulty)

> **Text 1**
> Urban planner Sofia Marek argues that converting street parking to
> protected bike lanes benefits local businesses, pointing to a corridor in
> her city where retail sales rose 15 percent after such a conversion.
>
> **Text 2**
> Economist David Chen notes that the corridor Marek cites also gained a
> light-rail stop in the same year. He does not dispute that bike lanes can
> help businesses, but he argues that attributing the sales increase to the
> bike lanes alone is unwarranted.

**Based on the texts, how would Chen (Text 2) most likely respond to Marek's
argument (Text 1)?**

- A) By rejecting the claim that bike lanes can benefit businesses
- B) By accepting the claim in general while questioning whether the cited
  evidence establishes it ✔
- C) By arguing that the corridor's sales actually declined after the
  conversion
- D) By proposing that light-rail stops are the only reliable way to raise
  retail sales

**Why the distractors fail:** A overstates the disagreement — Chen
explicitly concedes bike lanes can help. C contradicts Text 2 (Chen never
disputes the sales rise). D fabricates a position ("only reliable way") that
Text 2 gives Chen no basis to hold.

## Difficulty commentary

- **Easy:** clearly opposed or clearly aligned theses; the stem targets a
  claim stated in nearly identical terms in both texts.
- **Medium:** non-binary relationships (extends, complicates, partially
  confirms); agreement variants where the shared point is implicit in one
  text.
- **Hard:** both stances are hedged (X holds only under condition Y);
  distractors invert a condition or treat a difference in emphasis as a
  difference in position; response variants require inferring a reaction to
  a claim the author never directly addresses.

## Generation pitfalls

1. **Stance ambiguity.** Each author's position must be stateable in one
   sentence from that author's text alone. If you can't, the item is
   ungradable.
2. **Keys that pass the swap test.** Verify the correct answer becomes false
   if the text labels are swapped — if it still works, it isn't actually
   tracking stances.
3. **Straw-man texts.** Both positions should be reasonable. A cartoonishly
   weak Text 1 makes every "response" choice arguable.
4. **Relationship vocabulary drift.** "Refutes," "complicates," "extends,"
   and "illustrates" are different relationships — the key's label must be
   exact, and at least one distractor should be the plausible wrong label.
