# Form, Structure, and Sense

**Slug:** `form-structure-sense` · **Domain:** Standard English Conventions

## What it tests

Whether the student can supply the grammatically correct *form* of a word at
a blank: verb tense and agreement, pronoun case and number, plurals and
possessives, parallel structure, and logical comparisons. Unlike Boundaries
(punctuation), this type tests morphology and syntax — the sentence's own
signals (time markers, true subject, antecedent) determine exactly one form.

## Worked shape

One or two sentences of expository prose with a blank where the tested form
goes. The stem is the fixed *"Which choice completes the text so that it
conforms to the conventions of Standard English?"* The four choices are the
same word or phrase in different grammatical forms ("has yielded" /
"yielded" / "yields" / "had yielded"). Exactly one satisfies every planted
signal; each distractor violates exactly one, and each is producible by a
plausible misparse.

## Original example item (medium difficulty)

> The collection of oral histories gathered from ferry workers ______ a
> vivid record of how the harbor's daily rhythms have changed since the
> 1950s.

**Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of
Standard English?**

- A) provide
- B) provides ✔
- C) have provided
- D) providing

**Why the distractors fail:** The true subject is "the collection"
(singular), not "histories" — A and C agree with the nearer plural decoy
noun (and C also misaligns with the sentence's present-frame description).
D is a participle, leaving the sentence without a finite verb — a fragment.

## Difficulty commentary

- **Easy:** subject and verb adjacent; explicit time marker fixes tense;
  distractors sound wrong when read aloud.
- **Medium:** an intervening phrase hides the true subject; tense choice
  relates two events (past perfect vs simple past); two- or three-part
  parallel series.
- **Hard:** collective or indefinite subjects ("each of the samples", "the
  data"); subjunctive or perfect-progressive distinctions; comparison logic
  traps ("more than any other"); sentences carrying several signals at once
  where each distractor satisfies some and violates one.

## Generation pitfalls

1. **Missing signals.** If the sentence lacks a time marker, antecedent, or
   identifiable subject, more than one form is defensible. Plant the signals
   deliberately, then verify they determine one answer.
2. **Decoys that aren't decoys.** The intervening noun must actually tempt
   (adjacent to the blank, different number from the subject) — otherwise
   the item is easier than designed.
3. **Dialect-dependent judgments.** Avoid forms where regional or informal
   usage genuinely varies (e.g. collective-noun number in British vs
   American usage); test only conventions with a settled Standard English
   answer.
4. **Distractors violating two signals.** Keep each wrong choice to one
   clean violation, so the item diagnoses which signal the student missed.
5. **"Sounds right" keys.** Read the completed sentence aloud with the key;
   the correct form must be justified by a rule you can state, not by ear.
