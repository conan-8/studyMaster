# Inferences

**Slug:** `inferences` · **Domain:** Information and Ideas

## What it tests

Whether the student can draw the conclusion that a set of stated premises
most strongly supports — exactly one inferential step beyond the text, no
more. The calibration skill is the heart of the item: the correct answer
matches the evidence in scope, certainty, and direction.

## Worked shape

A 1–3 sentence passage lays out premises (observations, facts, a scenario)
and ends with a blank after a concluding marker ("Therefore, ...", "These
findings suggest that ______"). The stem is *"Which choice most logically
completes the text?"* or *"Which conclusion is most strongly supported by
the text?"* The four choices are candidate conclusions; the key follows from
the premises jointly with the least added assumption.

## Original example item (medium difficulty)

> When the borough of Marlow replaced its coin-fed parking meters with a
> pay-by-phone system, revenue from parking fees initially dropped 12
> percent. The drop occurred even though the number of parked vehicles,
> measured by weekly patrol counts, stayed constant. Within nine months,
> however, revenue had returned to its previous level as residents adopted
> the new system. Therefore, ______

**Which choice most logically completes the text?**

- A) the initial revenue drop was caused by a decline in parking in Marlow.
- B) pay-by-phone systems always produce higher long-term revenue than coin
  meters.
- C) the initial drop in revenue likely reflected residents' unfamiliarity
  with the new payment system rather than reduced parking. ✔
- D) weekly patrol counts are an unreliable way to measure parking behavior.

**Why the distractors fail:** A contradicts a stated premise (vehicle counts
stayed constant). B overreaches — one borough's recovery says nothing about
"always." D attacks a premise the passage treats as sound, and nothing in
the text supports that attack.

## Difficulty commentary

- **Easy:** two premises with one obvious joint implication; distractors are
  restatements or clearly unsupported.
- **Medium:** three premises; the inference requires combining at least two;
  one distractor is a near-miss overreach differing only in a qualifier.
- **Hard:** the inference depends on what the premises *rule out*; several
  choices are directionally right and only one is calibrated in scope and
  certainty; correlation-vs-causation discipline is often the discriminator.

## Generation pitfalls

1. **Premise leakage.** If one premise alone states (or nearly states) the
   conclusion, the item collapses into paraphrase-matching. The conclusion
   must require the premises *together*.
2. **Multiple entailed conclusions.** Check each distractor: if any is also
   logically supported, recalibrate it with a stronger modal or wider scope
   so it becomes an overreach.
3. **Keys stronger than the evidence.** The correct answer must not say
   "must" where the premises license only "likely."
4. **Real-world plausibility masquerading as support.** A choice can be true
   in the world and still wrong — but the *key* must never depend on
   outside knowledge either.
