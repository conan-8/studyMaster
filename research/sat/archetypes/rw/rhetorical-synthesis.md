# Rhetorical Synthesis

**Slug:** `rhetorical-synthesis` · **Domain:** Expression of Ideas

## What it tests

Whether the student can select and deploy *relevant* information from a set
of notes to accomplish a stated rhetorical goal. Three filters stack:
relevance (does the sentence use the notes that serve the goal?), accuracy
(does it state them faithfully?), and rhetorical execution (does it actually
perform the requested move — compare, emphasize, introduce — rather than
merely list facts?).

## Worked shape

A framing sentence ("While researching a topic, a student has taken the
following notes:") introduces 4–6 bulleted facts. Then a goal: *"The student
wants to <goal>. Which choice most effectively uses the relevant information
from the notes to accomplish this goal?"* The four choices are complete
sentences drawing on the notes; exactly one passes all three filters.

## Original example item (medium difficulty)

> While researching a topic, a student has taken the following notes:
>
> - The Miraflores water garden in Lima was completed in 2007.
> - The garden covers 8 hectares in the city's coastal district.
> - Its filtration wetlands clean approximately 2 million liters of
>   stormwater per day.
> - The garden was designed by landscape architect Inés Soublette.
> - It receives roughly 400,000 visitors per year.
>
> The student wants to emphasize the garden's environmental function to an
> audience unfamiliar with it. Which choice most effectively uses the
> relevant information from the notes to accomplish this goal?

- A) Completed in 2007, the Miraflores water garden covers 8 hectares in
  Lima's coastal district.
- B) Designed by Inés Soublette, Lima's Miraflores water garden is not just
  a public park: its filtration wetlands clean about 2 million liters of
  stormwater every day. ✔
- C) The Miraflores water garden, which receives roughly 400,000 visitors
  per year, is located in Lima's coastal district.
- D) Inés Soublette designed the Miraflores water garden, which was
  completed in 2007.

**Why the distractors fail:** A and D use only notes irrelevant to the
environmental-function goal (date, area, designer). C is accurate but
emphasizes popularity and location — a different goal. Only B both orients
an unfamiliar audience and puts the filtration fact at the sentence's
center of gravity.

## Difficulty commentary

- **Easy:** only one choice uses goal-relevant notes at all; distractors
  pursue other goals or distort facts obviously.
- **Medium:** two choices use relevant notes; they differ in accuracy or in
  whether the rhetorical move is actually performed; one tempting
  irrelevant note lures inclusion.
- **Hard:** multiple choices are relevant *and* accurate; discrimination
  turns on execution quality (explicit comparison vs side-by-side listing)
  or on a two-component goal ("emphasize X to an audience already familiar
  with Y") that distractors serve only half of.

## Generation pitfalls

1. **Polish as the cue.** If the correct sentence is simply the best-written
   one, the item tests taste. Keep all choices comparable in length and
   fluency so selection logic does the work.
2. **Fact drift in choices.** Every fact in every choice must match the
   notes verbatim — dates, names, magnitudes. A subtly wrong number in the
   *key* invalidates the item.
3. **Goals that fit all notes.** The goal must carve the notes into relevant
   and irrelevant subsets; if every note serves the goal, relevance cannot
   discriminate.
4. **Rhetorical moves that aren't checkable.** "Emphasize," "introduce,"
   "contrast," and "explain the significance of" must be operationalized:
   decide before drafting what textual feature counts as performing the
   move, and make sure only the key has it.
5. **Real-world fact dependency.** Invent the scenario or use genuinely
   generic topics — the student must need nothing beyond the notes.
