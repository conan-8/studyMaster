# Transitions

**Slug:** `transitions` · **Domain:** Expression of Ideas

## What it tests

Whether the student can identify the *logical relationship* between two
sentences and choose the connector that names it: contrast, cause-effect,
addition, exemplification, concession, or sequence. The content of both
sentences is fully given — only the logic between them is tested.

## Worked shape

Two sentences (30–90 words total) with a blank at the head of the second:
"______, ..." The stem is fixed: *"Which choice completes the text with the
most logical transition?"* Four transitional words or phrases; exactly one
names the true relationship. Grammar is never the cue — all four choices are
grammatical at the blank.

## Original example item (hard difficulty — surface cue vs deep logic)

> The ceramics studio at Halden College uses the same glazes, kilns, and
> firing schedules it has used for thirty years. ______, the pieces produced
> this decade are markedly more durable than those produced in the 1990s, a
> difference researchers attribute to a change in the local clay supply.

**Which choice completes the text with the most logical transition?**

- A) Similarly,
- B) For example,
- C) Nevertheless, ✔
- D) Accordingly,

**Why the distractors fail:** The shared materials and methods are a surface
cue pulling toward *similarly* — but the deep logic is concession: despite
everything being the same, the outcome differs. *For example* fails because
sentence 2 is not an instance of sentence 1's claim. *Accordingly* asserts a
result relationship, which is exactly backwards (the sameness did not cause
the improvement).

## Difficulty commentary

- **Easy:** an obvious binary relation (clear contrast or clear
  continuation); the four choices come from four different logical families.
- **Medium:** the relationship must be inferred from content; two choices
  share a family, forcing a polarity judgment; cause-vs-sequence ambiguity.
- **Hard:** subtle relations (concession vs contrast, elaboration vs
  example); a surface cue points at one relation while the deep logic
  requires another; sentence 2 partially fulfills an expectation set by
  sentence 1.

## Generation pitfalls

1. **Defensible runner-ups.** Read the pair with each distractor inserted.
   If any produces a relationship you could defend in an argument, rewrite
   sentence 2 so the target relation is exclusive.
2. **Grammar as an accidental cue.** All four choices must fit
   grammatically (same position, followed by a comma). If one choice is
   ungrammatical at the blank, the item stops testing logic.
3. **Underdetermined content.** If sentence 2 could relate to sentence 1 in
   several ways depending on real-world interpretation, the logic is not
   actually fixed — sharpen the content so one relation is forced.
4. **Transition redundancy.** The connector must add information; if the
   sentences already contain "but" or "because," the blank becomes a
   synonym exercise.
