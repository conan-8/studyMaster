# Words in Context

**Slug:** `words-in-context` · **Domain:** Craft and Structure

## What it tests

Whether the student can select the single most *logical and precise* word or
phrase for a blank, using the semantic clues in the surrounding sentence(s).
This is not raw vocabulary recall: the context must constrain the answer so
that a student who knows all four words can solve it from the passage alone.

## Worked shape

A 1–2 sentence passage (25–80 words) with one blank. The stem is fixed:
*"Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or
phrase?"* The four choices are single words of the same part of speech and
register. Exactly one satisfies every contextual constraint — a restatement,
example, contrast, or causal link planted near the blank. (A rarer variant
quotes a word in the passage and asks what it "most nearly means.")

## Original example item (hard difficulty — secondary sense)

> Archivist Petra Lindqvist does not merely preserve the ledgers donated to
> the municipal collection; she works to ______ their contents, transcribing
> faded entries and cross-referencing names so that the records can be
> searched and studied rather than simply stored.

**Which choice completes the text with the most logical and precise word or
phrase?**

- A) arrest
- B) recover ✔
- C) fashion
- D) table

**Why the distractors fail:** *Arrest* (stop), *fashion* (make), and *table*
(postpone) are all real verbs of similar register, but the context —
restoring faded, unusable records to usability — requires the "restore to a
usable state" sense that only *recover* carries here. The trap is that all
four are common words used in less-common senses, so surface familiarity
cannot discriminate.

## Difficulty commentary

- **Easy:** common words, direct clue adjacent to the blank (a restatement
  or a clear antonym signal), distractors obviously off.
- **Medium:** mid-frequency academic vocabulary; the clue requires the whole
  sentence; one distractor is a true near-synonym ruled out by a subtle
  mismatch.
- **Hard:** the trap is the *wrong sense of a plausible word* — the familiar
  meaning fails and a rarer one fits (or vice versa); clues are distributed
  across clauses and all four choices share register and theme.

## Generation pitfalls

1. **Topic-knowledge keys.** If only someone who knows the field can pick
   the word, the item is broken. The context alone must fix the sense.
2. **Arguable fits.** Read the sentence with each distractor inserted. If
   any produces a defensible meaning, replace it — there is exactly one best
   answer.
3. **Register mismatch giveaways.** If one choice is slangy or stilted
   against the others, style becomes the cue instead of meaning.
4. **Clue-free blanks.** A blank surrounded by neutral prose ("The
   researcher was very ______ about the results") admits several answers.
   Every item needs at least one explicit constraint.
5. **Collocation blindness.** A near-synonym distractor is only a good trap
   if its failure is identifiable (wrong collocation or wrong clause fit) —
   say the failure aloud when building it.
